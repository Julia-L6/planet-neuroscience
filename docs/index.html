    The Impact of Social Responsibility on the Adoption of Sustainable Development Practices on the Risk of Stock Price Collapse [version 2; peer review: 2 approved, 1 approved with reservations]

    Background Corporate social responsibility (CSR) and sustainable development practices have become increasingly important for firms seeking to address stakeholder expectations and improve corporate sustainability. However, their implications for capital-market risk, particularly stock price crash risk, remain unclear in emerging markets. This study examines the relationship between CSR and stock price crash risk and investigates whether the adoption of sustainable development practices mediates this relationship among Saudi-listed firms. Methods The study examines 95 non-financial companies listed on the Saudi Exchange in 2023, selected from an initial population of 231 listed companies based on predefined eligibility criteria. CSR and sustainable development practices were measured using multi-item instruments adapted from prior literature. Stock price crash risk was assessed using three commonly employed indicators: CRASHdummy, negative conditional skewness (NCSKEW), and down-to-up volatility (DUVOL). The hypothesized direct and indirect relationships were estimated using variance-based partial least squares structural equation modeling (PLS-SEM) with SmartPLS 4. Measurement-model reliability and validity were assessed before testing the structural paths and mediation effect. Results CSR was not significantly associated with stock price crash risk. In contrast, CSR was positively and significantly associated with the adoption of sustainable development practices. Sustainable development practices were also positively associated with stock price crash risk. The indirect relationship between CSR and stock price crash risk through sustainable development practices was statistically significant, supporting the proposed mediating role of sustainable development practices. Conclusions The findings indicate that CSR does not have a statistically significant direct relationship with stock price crash risk among Saudi-listed firms. Instead, its association with crash risk operates indirectly through firms’ adoption of sustainable development practices. These results highlight the importance of considering sustainability practices as a potential mechanism linking corporate social responsibility with capital-market outcomes in an emerging-market setting.

    Why Static Biomarkers Often Fall Short: Circadian Immune Coherence as a Missing Dimension in Immunotherapy [version 3; peer review: 1 approved with reservations, 1 not approved]

    Biomarkers such as PD-L1 expression and baseline inflammatory indices help inform cancer immunotherapy, but a single measurement provides limited information about how immune activity changes over time. Research on circadian regulation of immunity raises the question of whether the timing and pattern of these changes could provide additional information. In this Opinion Article, we use the term Circadian Immune Coherence (CIC) to describe a proposed framework for studying the reproducibility of approximately 24-hour immune rhythms and the stability of timing relationships between immune markers. It remains unclear whether these features have clinical value. Studying them would require repeated measurements at suitable intervals, since routine blood tests collected weeks apart cannot establish circadian rhythms. Early studies would need to define reproducible measures, account for influences such as sleep, medication, and treatment, and compare their findings with simpler approaches, including baseline measurements and longitudinal trends. The inconvenience and cost of repeated sampling would also need to be justified. CIC is therefore presented as a hypothesis for investigation. Its measurement, relevance to treatment outcomes, and possible role in guiding immunotherapy remain to be established.

    The Role of Artificial Intelligence in Reducing Maternal Mortality: A Systematic Review [version 1; peer review: awaiting peer review]

    Abstract* Introduction Maternal mortality remains a critical global health issue, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where the majority of deaths are preventable. Despite extensive efforts, progress in reducing maternal mortality has been slow and uneven. Recent advances in artificial intelligence (AI) and machine learning (ML) offer promising new strategies for addressing persistent gaps in maternal healthcare, particularly through early risk identification and optimized clinical decision-making. Materials and methods A comprehensive literature search was conducted across PubMed, Scopus, ScienceDirect, ProQuest, and Medline May 2020 to June 2025, following PRISMA guidelines. Registered in PROSPERO with CRD420251051696. Eligible studies included randomized controlled trials, cohort, or case-control studies investigating AI-based approaches to reduce maternal mortality among pregnant women. Data extracted included study characteristics, AI model details, and clinical outcomes. Study quality was assessed using the ROBINS-I tools. Results Eleven studies met the inclusion criteria, encompassing diverse settings and populations. AI and ML models, such as random forest, CatBoost, Naïve Bayes, and xgboost, were used to predict outcomes including obstetric complications, gestational diabetes, sepsis, hypertensive disorders, and postpartum hemorrhage. Many models demonstrated high predictive accuracy (AUC > 0.80), enabling early identification of high-risk pregnancies and supporting timely interventions. Integrating clinical, laboratory, and social determinants of health data improved model performance. However, challenges remain regarding data quality, infrastructure, ethical considerations, and model interpretability. Conclusion AI have significant potential to reduce maternal mortality by facilitating early risk stratification and personalized care. To realize this potential, future research should focus on prospective studies, integration of multimodal data, and development of explainable, equitable AI models. Collaborative efforts across disciplines and stakeholders are essential to address implementation barriers and achieve global maternal health targets.

    Platform Content Moderation and Freedom of Expression in Southeast Asia: A Systematic Literature Review [version 1; peer review: awaiting peer review]

    Background Big Tech platform self-regulation and national anti-disinformation laws jointly govern political speech in Southeast Asia, a region without a supranational regulatory framework, yet their interaction has not been systematically synthesized. This review examined how they interact in shaping political disinformation and freedom of expression in the region from 2015 to 2025. Methods Following PRISMA 2020, we searched Scopus, ScienceDirect, SAGE Journals, and JSTOR in April–May 2026 for English-language, peer-reviewed studies. Of 3,886 records, 35 studies were included. Quality was appraised with the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT) 2018, and findings were synthesized thematically around three research questions. A large language model assisted screening, summarization, and initial appraisal, with all outputs checked by the authors. The methods were registered retrospectively on the Open Science Framework (doi:10.17605/OSF.IO/BGXHW). Results The studies (2015–2026; 74.3% qualitative) were concentrated in Malaysia and in studies without a single-country focus. Three themes were identified: platform–state interaction (n = 13), regulatory gaps and digital rights safeguards (n = 15), and discursive patterns in platform policies (n = 8). States used legal, technical, and administrative pressure to secure platform compliance, while platforms adapted their moderation largely on pragmatic and commercial grounds. Vaguely defined laws, their misuse, state-linked manipulation, and chilling effects were widely documented, whereas evidence on changes to Terms of Service and on benchmarking against international freedom of expression standards was limited. Platform policy language was strategically ambiguous, and moderation increasingly operated through visibility. Conclusions Ambiguity in both state law and platform policy may function as a feature of governance rather than only as a drafting failure. Improving the governance of online speech in the region is likely to require independent oversight of enforcement, transparency about moderation practice, and co-regulatory arrangements with clearly defined safeguards. Future research should address the underrepresentation of Vietnam, Cambodia, and the Philippines.

    Exploring Beyond Earth: Reconceptualizing Space Exploration as a Human Rights Imperative [version 1; peer review: awaiting peer review]

    Background Humanity’s transition from episodic exploration to long-duration human habitation poses a fundamental structural challenge to public international law. International human rights law developed within a terrestrial matrix predicated on sovereign territorial jurisdiction, geographical borders, and the natural availability of an unmanaged biological biosphere. In outer space, where national appropriation is prohibited under Article II of the 1967 Outer Space Treaty, human existence depends unconditionally on engineered life-support infrastructure and autonomous algorithmic mediation. Methods This study employs a doctrinal legal methodology, analysing primary instruments such as the 1967 Outer Space Treaty, the ICCPR, and the ICESCR, alongside the Human Rights Committee’s and the ICJ’s extraterritorial human rights jurisprudence. The analysis maps the jurisdictional nexus between Article VIII and Article VI of the OST and the extraterritorial scope of human rights obligations; it then synthesises emerging neuro-rights scholarship to construct a normative framework for human agency in space habitats. Results and Conclusions The article demonstrates that existing human rights conventions possess extraterritorial application to space habitats through the jurisdictional nexus of Article VIII and the special responsibility regime of Article VI of the OST. On this basis, it develops a four-pillar model: (1) Equitable Space Participation; (2) Juridical Ubiquity; (3) Intergenerational Stewardship; and (4) Cognitive Integrity and Human Agency. Focusing on the fourth pillar, the paper establishes the principle of infrastructural dependency as the basis for heightened human rights obligations: where meaningful physical exit is impossible, structural control over essential survival systems gives inhabitants profound leverage. By analysing the non-derogable forum internum under Article 18 of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights alongside emerging neuro-rights jurisprudence, the article formulates the Principle of Human Agency in Outer Space and articulates legal standards of necessity, proportionality, procedural transparency, and contestability to prevent extraterrestrial habitats from becoming domains of private corporate coercion.

    Reflection as attitude, not competence: a document-led case study of outcome-based education in an Indonesian economics teacher education program [version 1; peer review: awaiting peer review]

    Background Outcome-based education (OBE) is the mandated curriculum paradigm in Indonesian higher education, yet critics warn that outcome frameworks favor what is easiest to specify and measure. This study examined how technical, reflective and adaptive competence are represented in the 2025 curriculum of an Economic Education program and how program staff describe its enactment. Methods A single qualitative case study was conducted at the Faculty of Economics and Business, Universitas Negeri Semarang. The primary data source was the program’s 2025 curriculum document, analyzed through directed content analysis with a Technical, Reflective (reasoning and disposition) and Adaptive coding frame. Two semi-structured interviews with program staff served as explanatory data and were analyzed thematically. Findings were triangulated across sources. Results Technical and adaptive codes appeared in eight of the nine program learning outcomes. Reflective content appeared in seven, but in six of these it took the form of an attitudinal statement (commitment, integrity, ethics) appended to a technical outcome; reflective reasoning that could be demonstrated and assessed appeared in three, and the word “reflective” in one. The document explicitly ranked scientific and pedagogical outcomes as dominant and attitude outcomes as supporting. Reflection was named in assessment principles and in one portfolio rubric, while stage progression relied on grades and grade-point thresholds. Both informants described implementation as partial; they diverged on the adequacy of institutional support, and one reported that final grades were still awarded as single aggregates rather than by course outcome. Conclusions In this program, reflective competence is written into the curriculum mainly as an attitude rather than as an assessable capability, while assessment infrastructure remains grade-based. Findings are limited to one program, one document and two informants, and are offered as an analytic case.

    Geometric and algebraic inference in humans and neural networks

    arXiv:2611.00387v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: Humans possess a remarkable ability to draw inferences from limited data. While some inferences are made intuitively from past experience, others require online reasoning based on novel information given in context. Here, we formalize these strategies, which we term geometric and algebraic inference, and study their computational tradeoffs in humans and neural networks. The geometric strategy gradually shapes representational geometry to reflect underlying relational structure, making subsequent inference cheap. The algebraic strategy flexibly attends to and reasons over novel relations given in context, but costs additional computation at inference time. We identify opposing signatures of these two strategies in transitive inference, a domain widely studied across species, and characterize the conditions under which each is observed. Our framework predicts that the geometric strategy should be dominant when relations are familiar, whereas the algebraic should emerge to support de novo inferences drawn in context. Multiple experiments demonstrate these opposing signatures in humans, neural networks trained from scratch, and large language models (LLMs). Causal interventions in the neural networks reveal that the geometric and algebraic strategies depend on representational geometry and selective attention mechanisms, respectively, and reproduce the effects of analogous neurobiological disruptions in humans. Our framework unifies these effects with disparate phenomena, including content effects observed in human and LLM reasoning and data-distribution effects observed in machine learning. Our findings reconcile competing views of cognitive flexibility that emphasize either representational geometry or algebraic manipulation by showing that each can have unique strengths, and can characterize different aspects of both human and neural network behavior.

    A Planar Switching Neuron Shares Logarithmic First-Spike Latency with Its Three-Dimensional Parent But Not Its Onset Mechanism

    arXiv:2611.01632v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: Can a reduced neuron model reproduce first-spike latency scaling while changing how a stimulus initiates sustained firing? We compare a piecewise-linear planar Neurosimilator with its three-dimensional parent. Both exhibit logarithmic latency dependence over the investigated input intervals, with different fitted coefficients. The mechanisms differ. In the parent, sustained spiking and a stable resting focus coexist in a spiking--rest bistability regime. An abrupt step accesses spiking, while slow stimulation can retain rest at the same final input. Its step-induced firing threshold is therefore an operational basin-access threshold below the saddle-node limit point, not a loss of equilibrium stability. In the planar model, the resting equilibrium instead reaches a switching boundary while its subthreshold eigenvalues remain negative. We derive the planar latency law from this boundary approach. The model generates repetitive excursions without an imposed reset and admits exact trajectories within six switching regions. Similar measured latency scaling thus coexists with different mechanisms of access to firing. Validating a reduced model's mechanism of excitability requires examination of stimulus protocol and attractor access alongside equilibrium structure and spike timing.

    arXiv:2611.03071v1 Announce Type: new Abstract: Dynamic functional connectivity (dFC) reflects ongoing reconfiguration of large-scale brain networks, but the temporal organization and network processes underlying spontaneous state transitions remain poorly understood.     The (Butter)fly Dream: Linear Separability of World-Model Rollouts in a Connectome-Constrained Visual Model

    arXiv:2611.01866v1 Announce Type: cross Abstract: Zhuangzi dreamt he was a butterfly and, on waking, could not tell which was the dream. We ask a version of his question of a model of the fly brain: placed inside a world generated by a world model, can a fixed brain tell that the world is a dream? We treat it as a question about what a connectome-constrained model trained on one task makes available beyond that task: do its responses make the defects of a dream linearly decodable? The observer is a published model of the fly visual system trained only for optic-flow estimation; reality is the MPI-Sintel movie on the model's 721-column retina; dreams are continuations of five real frames by three small world models (a persistence random walk, an MDN-RNN and an RSSM) at three temperatures. The judge is a classifier two-sample test on temporal statistics of cell-type responses, with sequence-level cross-validation. In the trained observer's responses all nine dreams are separable from reality (AUC 0.85-0.99, mean over 50 trained models), in those of the same network before training much less so (0.58-0.75 for the learned world models; paired difference 0.20-0.37), equally on sequences the observer never saw, and in an order unrelated to pixel error. Because these AUCs are near ceiling, we add graded probes made of each window's own real frames with only their order broken.
